EU AI Act lab – my four client cases

16 November 2026 · Friedrich Tschoepke

Four hidden client briefs written for the partner exchange, plus the private answer key. The briefs below carry no category labels; the order is mixed and the headings are neutral, so the tier cannot be read off the title.

Part 1 – Client briefs (shared with the partner)
Client A – Funding consultancy

A 40-person consultancy helps manufacturing SMEs find public R&D grants. Consultants lose hours screening programmes by hand. They want a tool that takes company data (sector, size, revenue, project description) and ranks the funding programmes with the best fit, including a "fit score" and deadline alerts. The data comes from the CRM and public programme databases. The ranking goes to the consultant, who decides what to pitch to the client. Management would like to advertise the score on the website as "AI-verified eligibility".

Client B – Customer service outsourcer

A contact centre operator with 600 agents has high sick leave and turnover. It wants a "wellbeing assistant" that analyses agents' voice tone during calls and webcam images at the workstation to detect stress and frustration in real time. Team leads see a dashboard per agent; the signals should also feed into shift planning and coaching talks. The same tool scores the customer's mood on each call. HR says nobody is fired on the basis of the tool; a team lead always looks at the data first.

Client C – Hotel group

A regional hotel group with 12 properties misses about 30% of booking calls. It wants a voice assistant for the booking hotline that answers questions, checks availability and takes reservations. Marketing insists it should "sound like a real receptionist, with a name". It uses the booking system, FAQs and the caller's reservation history. In addition, marketing wants promotional videos featuring a synthetic presenter who looks like a real hotel employee. Complex requests are handed over to staff.

Client D – Vocational training provider

A private training provider runs publicly funded reskilling courses; there are three times as many applicants as places. It wants a system that scores online aptitude tests and motivation letters and produces a ranked admission list per course. Inputs are test answers, CVs, letters and previous education records. Admissions staff receive the list and "normally confirm the top 20"; they have about two minutes per applicant. The client calls it "just a pre-sorting aid". The tool comes from an EdTech vendor, but the client wants to retrain it on its own past admission data and run it under its own brand.

Part 2 – Private answer key

Not shared with the partner before the debrief.

Case	Category	Why	Borderline argument
A	Minimal	Ranks programmes for companies, not natural persons, and touches no Annex III area.	"Scoring" sounds risky but concerns legal entities. Parallel issues: "AI-verified eligibility" is a misleading claim (consumer/unfair competition law), GDPR for contact persons in the CRM.
B	Prohibited	Emotion recognition from biometric data in the workplace, Art. 5(1)(f).	The client will invoke the medical/safety exception; general wellbeing monitoring does not fall under it. Human review does not cure a prohibition. The customer-mood part is not prohibited (customers are not workers), but it is emotion recognition with its own duties. Redesign: drop biometric inference on agents, use aggregated, anonymised workload metrics and voluntary surveys.
C	Limited risk	Art. 50(1): callers must be told they are talking to an AI. Art. 50(4): the synthetic presenter is a deepfake and must be disclosed.	"Sounds like a real receptionist" directly conflicts with the disclosure duty. If the avatar copies a real employee: consent and personality rights. Reservation history: GDPR.
D	High-risk	Annex III no. 3(a): determining access or admission to education and vocational training.	The client argues "preparatory task" under Art. 6(3); that fails because the system profiles natural persons. Two minutes per applicant is not effective human oversight (Art. 14). Retraining and rebranding can make the client the provider (Art. 25). FRIA under Art. 27 is plausible because of the public funding; that is a point for the legal team.
Notes
Case D is the best candidate for the stretch task (implementation roadmap), because the provider/deployer question is genuinely open there.
The classifications rest on the text of the Regulation and were not looked up. Application deadlines were most recently moved by the Digital Omnibus; check the current position before citing any dates in the approval pack.
