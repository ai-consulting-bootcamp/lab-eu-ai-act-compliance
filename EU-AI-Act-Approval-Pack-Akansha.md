## PART 1 — CLIENT BRIEFS AS RECEIVED

**Case 1**
A large retail company wants to improve employee retention across its stores. It plans to install AI-enabled cameras in staff areas and use facial and behavioural signals to estimate each employee's emotional state during the working day — expressions, posture, voice characteristics, and other observable signals to determine whether an employee appears stressed, frustrated, disengaged or satisfied. Store managers would receive individual employee profiles and could use the scores when deciding who receives additional supervision, performance discussions, or other workplace interventions. Managers could override the system's recommendations, but the AI scores would remain part of the employee assessment record.

**Case 2**
A national recruitment company wants to reduce the time recruiters spend screening applications for large-volume vacancies. It plans to introduce an AI system that analyses CVs, application answers, and relevant professional information, then ranks candidates against the requirements of each vacancy. Recruiters would see a recommended shortlist and an explanation of the factors that influenced the ranking. A recruiter would review the shortlist before candidates are invited to interviews, and recruiters could reject or add candidates themselves. The company wants to use the system across several recruitment offices and retain the assessment results for future hiring campaigns.

**Case 3**
A telecommunications company wants to add an AI assistant to its customer service website to handle routine questions about contracts, billing, network coverage, and technical support. Customers would type questions into a chat window and receive responses generated by the AI assistant. The assistant could retrieve information from the company's approved knowledge base and escalate more complex issues to a human service agent. The company wants the assistant to communicate in a natural, conversational style so customers can complete simple support requests without waiting for an employee.

**Case 4**
A mid-sized architecture company wants to help its design teams work more efficiently preparing internal project materials. It plans to use an AI writing assistant to turn meeting notes and project summaries into first drafts of internal project updates, brainstorming notes, and presentation outlines. Employees would review and edit the generated material before using it. The system would not make decisions about employees, customers, or suppliers, and the company would not use the generated text as an automated basis for contracts or other legal commitments. The company wants to make the tool available to its design and project-management teams as a productivity aid.

## PART 3 — APPROVAL PACK

## Executive Summary

Four proposed AI systems were reviewed against EU AI Act risk tiers. The portfolio splits across the full risk spectrum: one system is a **prohibited practice** and cannot proceed in its current form (Case 1 — workplace emotion monitoring); one is a **high-risk system** that is approvable once specific Article 9/10 controls are implemented and verified (Case 2 — CV screening and ranking); one requires a **targeted transparency fix** rather than a redesign (Case 3 — customer service assistant); and one carries **no AI Act obligations** as scoped (Case 4 — internal writing assistant).

The pattern worth flagging up front: good human-oversight design does not rescue a system that fails at the category level. Case 2's recruiter-review-with-explanation is genuinely strong design and is exactly why it lands in "approvable with controls" rather than "deny." Case 1 has a comparable-looking override step, but it sits downstream of a banned inference — the prohibition attaches to the inference itself, not to what's done with its output, so no amount of human review upstream changes the classification. This distinction should anchor the client conversation.

## Case 1 — Workplace Emotion Monitoring (Retail)

**Proposal:** A large retail company wants to improve employee retention across its stores. It plans to install AI-enabled cameras in staff areas and use facial and behavioural signals to estimate each employee's emotional state during the working day — expressions, posture, voice characteristics, and other observable signals to determine whether an employee appears stressed, frustrated, disengaged or satisfied. Store managers would receive individual employee profiles and could use the scores when deciding who receives additional supervision, performance discussions, or other workplace interventions. Managers could override the system's recommendations, but the AI scores would remain part of the employee assessment record.

**Category: Prohibited — Article 5(1)(f).**
Article 5(1)(f) bans AI systems that infer emotions in the workplace, subject only to a narrow safety or medical exception. This system doesn't qualify: the stated purpose is retention and supervision, and the fact that scores are written into a permanent HR record — irrespective of whether a manager overrides a specific recommendation — makes the exposure worse, not better. This is a category-level bar, not a controls gap.

**Architecture as proposed:** Camera feeds → facial/posture/voice affect model → per-employee emotional-state profile → manager dashboard → supervision/performance decisions, with scores retained regardless of override.

**Roles:** Camera/affect-recognition system vendor = provider; retail company = deployer; underlying affect-recognition model supplier = sub-provider.

**Decision: Deny and redesign.** No set of controls brings this into compliance — the practice itself is barred.

**Redesign path:** Remove emotion/affect inference entirely. If the underlying business goal is genuinely retention, a lawful alternative uses aggregated, non-biometric analytics — attendance patterns, shift-swap frequency, exit-interview themes, engagement-survey trends — reviewed by HR at the team or store level, never inferring an individual's emotional state from biometric signal.

## Case 2 — CV Screening and Ranking (Recruitment)

**Proposal:** A national recruitment company wants to reduce the time recruiters spend screening applications for large-volume vacancies. It plans to introduce an AI system that analyses CVs, application answers, and relevant professional information, then ranks candidates against the requirements of each vacancy. Recruiters would see a recommended shortlist and an explanation of the factors that influenced the ranking. A recruiter would review the shortlist before candidates are invited to interviews, and recruiters could reject or add candidates themselves. The company wants to use the system across several recruitment offices and retain the assessment results for future hiring campaigns.

**Category: High-risk — Annex III(4)(a), recruitment and candidate ranking.**
This is a squarely-covered Annex III use. The design has real strengths — explained rankings rather than a black box, and human review positioned before any consequential decision — but strong design moves a system toward "approvable," not toward "no obligations." The retained-results detail introduces a separate data-governance question: was candidate data collected with future-campaign reuse in mind, and could the model re-rank someone against a different vacancy using data gathered for the original one.

**Architecture as proposed:** CVs + application answers + professional info → ranking model with factor explanations → recommended shortlist → recruiter review (reject/add authority) → interview invitations → results retained for future campaigns.

**Roles:** Recruitment company (system developer/market-placer) = provider; each recruitment office/client = deployer; any licensed CV-parsing/NLP component = vendor.

**Decision: Approve with controls**, conditional on:
- Article 9 risk management system, implemented and documented
- Article 10 data governance and bias/fairness testing across protected characteristics
- Conformity assessment and technical documentation completed before deployment
- Logging and record-keeping sufficient to reconstruct individual ranking decisions
- A written retention and reuse policy for stored assessment results, with a defined limit on cross-campaign reuse
- Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment before scaling across multiple offices

Approval is conditional on these requirements being implemented and verified before go-live — this is not a rubber stamp on the current design.

## Case 3 — Customer Service Assistant (Telecom)

**Proposal:** A telecommunications company wants to add an AI assistant to its customer service website to handle routine questions about contracts, billing, network coverage, and technical support. Customers would type questions into a chat window and receive responses generated by the AI assistant. The assistant could retrieve information from the company's approved knowledge base and escalate more complex issues to a human service agent. The company wants the assistant to communicate in a natural, conversational style so customers can complete simple support requests without waiting for an employee.

**Category: Limited risk / transparency — Article 50(1).**
No Annex III trigger; this is customer support, not a high-risk domain. The live issue is disclosure: any AI system intended to interact directly with natural persons must make that clear unless it's obvious from context. A deliberately natural, human-sounding style is a legitimate UX goal, but it's also exactly the situation where disclosure can't be assumed — the brief describes no disclosure step.

**Architecture as proposed:** Customer query → retrieval from approved knowledge base → LLM-generated response → escalation to human agent for complex issues.

**Roles:** AI assistant developer = provider; telecommunications company = deployer; underlying LLM/API supplier = vendor.

**Decision: Approve with controls**, conditional on:
- Clear, early AI disclosure at the start of the chat session (e.g., "You are chatting with an AI assistant")
- Guardrails against hallucinated contract terms, given the subject matter
- A visibly available human-escalation path, not one gated on the customer explicitly asking
- A GDPR/privacy review to the extent customer account data is processed

Transparency is the primary control here, but the operational safeguards are worth insisting on — incorrect telecom advice (billing, contract terms) can cause real customer harm even without an AI Act trigger.

## Case 4 — Internal Writing Assistant (Architecture Firm)

**Proposal:** A mid-sized architecture company wants to help its design teams work more efficiently preparing internal project materials. It plans to use an AI writing assistant to turn meeting notes and project summaries into first drafts of internal project updates, brainstorming notes, and presentation outlines. Employees would review and edit the generated material before using it. The system would not make decisions about employees, customers, or suppliers, and the company would not use the generated text as an automated basis for contracts or other legal commitments. The company wants to make the tool available to its design and project-management teams as a productivity aid..

**Category: Minimal risk.**
The brief itself rules out every pathway that would move this into a higher tier: no consequential decisions about people, no contract automation, human review built into the workflow. No Annex III area, no biometric or emotion element, no manipulative pattern.

**Architecture as proposed:** Meeting notes/project summaries → LLM drafting assistant → first-draft internal materials → employee review and edit before use.

**Roles:** Underlying LLM/writing-assistant vendor = provider; architecture firm = deployer.

**Decision: Approve.** No AI Act–specific obligations apply at the deployer level. Worth a light-touch internal note on the confidentiality of client and project data flowing into a third-party model — a data-handling concern, not an AI Act one — and a reminder that any transparency obligations for general-purpose AI models sit with the model provider, not this deployment.

**5. Closing note — What changed after the client discussion?**

Nothing changed substantively, and that's itself the useful signal. A four-for-four match on a client-authored, deliberately unlabeled brief set — including correctly separating a *prohibited* case from a *high-risk* one that shared a superficially similar "manager/recruiter can override" feature — indicates the classification logic held up under a genuine blind test, not just a rehearsed one. The one place the discussion earned its keep was Case 1: walking through *why* the manager-override detail doesn't change the outcome (the prohibition attaches to the inference step, not to what's done with its output) confirmed the client's own reasoning for including that detail, rather than surfacing a gap in the original analysis. In a live engagement, this is the outcome you want before sign-off — agreement reached through independent verification, not negotiation.

## Reinforce

**Borderline arguments / counter-arguments:**

- *Case 1 (Retail emotion monitoring):* The client could argue the cameras are really a **safety** measure — catching burnout before it causes a workplace incident — which would edge toward the Article 5(1)(f) exception. This argument doesn't hold on the facts as given: the brief ties the scores to supervision and performance discussions, not to a safety protocol, and the exception is narrow by design.
- *Case 2 (CV ranking):* The recruitment company could argue that because a human always reviews the shortlist before any reject/invite decision, the system is "advisory only" and shouldn't count as high-risk at all. Annex III classification turns on the system's *intended purpose* (ranking candidates for employment decisions), not on whether a human happens to sit downstream of it — advisory framing doesn't change the tier, only the adequacy of oversight within that tier.
- *Case 3 (Telecom chatbot):* The company could argue that most customers "obviously" know they're talking to a bot given how common these assistants are now, so explicit disclosure is unnecessary. This is a weaker argument than it sounds — Article 50(1)'s "obvious from context" test is about the specific interaction, not general market familiarity, and a deliberately natural conversational style cuts against obviousness rather than supporting it.
- *Case 4 (Architecture writing assistant):* Someone could argue that because outputs get used in client-facing presentations, the tool is closer to a decision-support system than a pure internal draft. This doesn't move the classification — the AI Act cares about decisions *about* people (employment, credit, essential services, etc.), not the downstream audience of a document — but it's worth confirming the client isn't quietly using outputs for anything closer to a proposal or contract document, which would be a scope question rather than a category one.

**Where legal should verify the final interpretation:**

- Case 1: Confirm with legal whether any jurisdiction-specific labor-law safety obligations could plausibly support a safety-exception argument — this is the one case where local employment law nuance could matter beyond the AI Act text.
- Case 2: Legal should confirm the data-retention and reuse position (using stored results across future hiring campaigns) against both the AI Act's data governance requirements and applicable employment/data-protection law in each jurisdiction where the recruitment offices operate.
- Case 3: Legal should confirm what counts as "obvious from context" under national transparency guidance, since Article 50 leaves some interpretive room the client may want more certainty on before launch.
- Case 4: Legal should confirm the boundary between "internal drafting aid" and any adjacent use (e.g., if outputs start feeding into client proposals or contractual language) stays within the client's stated scope.

**Next operational artifact per case:**

- Case 1: A redesign brief for the non-biometric retention-analytics alternative, replacing the current camera-based architecture doc.
- Case 2: A Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment template, scoped specifically to this ranking use case, plus the written data-retention policy.
- Case 3: A transparency-notice pattern (the exact disclosure copy and where it appears in the chat flow) for the telecom's dev team to implement directly.
- Case 4: A short internal data-handling guideline for staff, covering what project/client information is and isn't appropriate to paste into the tool.

## Stretch — Mini Implementation Roadmap: Case 2 (CV Screening and Ranking)

**Before market placement (provider — recruitment company):**
- Complete conformity assessment and technical documentation for the ranking system, including risk-management documentation under Article 9.
- Run bias and accuracy testing disaggregated across protected characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, disability status), using representative applicant data rather than a convenience sample.
- Finalize instructions for use covering intended operating conditions, known limitations, and guidance on interpreting the ranking-factor explanations shown to recruiters.

**Before first use (deployer — each recruitment office / employer client):**
- Stand up logging sufficient to reconstruct any individual candidate's ranking and the factors behind it.
- Complete a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment before rolling the system out across multiple offices.
- Set clear criteria for when recruiters should override the ranking, and track override rates as an ongoing oversight signal so the review step doesn't erode into a rubber stamp.
- Finalize the written retention and reuse policy for stored assessment results before any cross-campaign reuse begins.

**Evidence to request from the vendor (if any component is third-party):**
- Documentation of what CV-parsing or NLP components are licensed rather than built in-house, and what training data underlies them.
- Any existing bias or accuracy testing the vendor has already run on the parsing/ranking components, so the recruitment company isn't starting from zero.
- Confirmation of data provenance for any third-party signal feeding the ranking (e.g., qualification-verification services), and whether that data was collected in a way that supports its use in this context.
