## Decision rules (reference while filling this in)

- **Prohibited:** deny as proposed, then offer a redesigned legal alternative.
- **High-risk:** do not deny automatically — approval depends on clear controls, oversight, and documentation.
- **Limited risk / transparency:** approval depends on clear disclosures and safe deployment boundaries.
- **Minimal risk:** approval is usually possible, but flag any parallel issues (GDPR, consumer protection, misleading claims).

## Approval pack draft (3 pages max, fill after the table above)

### Executive summary

Across the four proposals reviewed, risk splits clearly along one line: systems that infer something about a *person's internal state* draw the most scrutiny, while systems that inspect *parts or process data* stay clean. Case 1 (floor-camera fatigue scoring) cannot be launched as designed — it infers workplace emotion outside the AI Act's narrow safety exception. Case 2 (promotion/termination scoring) and Case 3 (driver drowsiness detection) are both high-risk but launchable, provided the client accepts real oversight and disclosure obligations rather than treating them as a rubber-stamp. Case 4 (cosmetic panel vision QC) is minimal risk and ready to proceed as proposed. A recurring theme worth flagging to the client group generally: several of these systems were built on top of *existing* camera or data infrastructure (QA cameras, chat-platform licenses) without re-evaluating what a new *inferential* use of that data triggers under the AI Act — that gap is worth a standing checklist item for any future AI layer added to existing sensors.

### Case-by-case detail

**Case 1 — Production-floor fatigue/engagement monitoring**
*Brief:* Automotive parts manufacturer wants to add facial-expression and posture analysis to existing QA cameras, producing daily per-operator fatigue summaries used by supervisors for coaching and for prioritizing voluntary overtime offers.
*Category:* Prohibited — Article 5(1)(f). The AI Act bans inferring emotions of natural persons in the workplace, with a narrow exception limited to safety or medical purposes. Coaching conversations and overtime allocation are performance/scheduling uses, not safety-critical interventions, so the exception doesn't apply. The existing QA-camera notice doesn't cover this new inferential use either, so there's a separate transparency gap even setting the prohibition aside.
*Architecture & roles:* Provider — the AI analytics vendor supplying the emotion/fatigue-detection layer. Deployer — the plant. No third-party vendor beyond the analytics provider; the cameras themselves are pre-existing QA hardware.
*Compliance implications:* None available — this cannot be brought into compliance by adding controls, since the prohibition is categorical, not risk-managed.
*Decision:* **Deny as proposed.**
*Redesign option:* Remove facial/posture emotion inference entirely. If the client's real goal is safety-related fatigue detection (e.g., preventing micro-sleep near hazardous machinery), that narrower use may qualify for the safety exception — but it would need to be scoped strictly to a safety-trigger alert, with explicit worker notice and consent, and firewalled from any use in overtime allocation, coaching, or performance review.

**Case 2 — Promotion and termination-risk scoring**
*Brief:* Automotive component manufacturer wants a quarterly composite score per employee, combining productivity/quality/attendance data with a "collaboration signal" derived from chat tone and responsiveness, used as the primary input for promotion nominations and as an early termination-risk flag.
*Category:* High-risk — Annex III, employment and worker-management. AI used to support promotion and termination-adjacent decisions falls directly in this category, regardless of the chat-tone signal's secondary emotion-inference flavor.
*Architecture & roles:* Provider — the chat-platform vendor (via its bundled "collaboration insights" feature) plus the internal HR analytics team building the composite score. Deployer — the employer. No separate third-party vendor beyond the chat platform.
*Compliance implications:* Requires a risk-management system, bias testing on the composite score (particularly the chat-tone signal, which is the least validated input), technical documentation, and — critically — advance notice to employees and worker representatives before deployment (Article 26). "Human review" as currently designed (manager reads score, writes recommendation) needs to become genuine oversight with real ability to deviate from the score, not a formality. A GDPR DPIA is also likely required given the profiling and its employment impact.
*Decision:* **Approve with controls**, contingent on: (1) informing employees and consulting worker representatives before rollout, (2) a documented bias audit of the chat-tone signal specifically, (3) evidence that manager overrides happen in practice, not just in policy. Recommend the client consider dropping the chat-tone signal from the *termination-risk* use case specifically, since that's the highest-stakes, least-validated application of it.

**Case 3 — In-cabin driver drowsiness detection**
*Brief:* Tier-1 automotive supplier wants an in-cabin camera system that estimates driver drowsiness/distraction from eyelid closure, head position, and gaze, triggering an audible + dashboard alert. Processing is local-only, nothing leaves the vehicle, and the feature is disclosed in the owner's manual and via a dashboard icon.
*Category:* High-risk — Annex I, treated conservatively. The Article 5(1)(f) safety exception clearly covers the emotion/state inference itself, so this is not prohibited. But drowsiness/attention-warning systems are increasingly mandated features under EU vehicle type-approval safety rules (GSR 2019/2144), and safety components of already-type-approved products can be pulled into Annex I independent of the emotion-recognition exception. Counter-argument worth flagging: local-only processing and existing manual/icon disclosure look more like a limited-risk/transparency profile — this is genuinely borderline and should go to legal for confirmation.
*Architecture & roles:* Provider — the Tier-1 supplier building the module (the OEM may also count as a provider if it integrates or rebrands the system under its own name). Deployer — ambiguous between the OEM at integration and the vehicle owner/fleet operator in actual use; worth clarifying with legal.
*Compliance implications:* If Annex I applies, the client needs conformity assessment (likely already satisfied by the existing GSR type-approval process, avoiding duplicate work), technical documentation, and post-market monitoring. If the transparency-only reading prevails instead, the existing manual/icon disclosure already satisfies the obligation.
*Decision:* **Approve with controls**, treated conservatively as high-risk pending legal/type-approval team confirmation of how the GSR conformity assessment maps onto AI Act Annex I obligations.

**Case 4 — Cosmetic panel machine-vision QC**
*Brief:* Metal-stamping supplier wants a machine-vision system on the cosmetic panel inspection line, comparing each panel against a reference image library and auto-routing clear passes/fails, with only borderline-confidence cases pulled for manual technician review.
*Category:* Minimal risk. The system inspects parts, not people — no individual is scored or evaluated, and no personal data is processed. The client's own scoping (cosmetic panels only, explicitly excluding the structural-bracket line) keeps this clear of any functional-safety argument that might otherwise apply to structural or safety-critical components.
*Architecture & roles:* Provider — the machine-vision AI vendor. Deployer — the stamping plant. No personal-data processing vendor involved.
*Compliance implications:* None under the AI Act. Standard QA traceability recordkeeping should continue as a normal quality-system requirement, independent of AI Act obligations.
*Decision:* **Approve as proposed.** Flag for the client that if the vision system's scope is ever extended to the structural-bracket line, this classification should be revisited given the closer tie to vehicle functional-safety standards.

- **What changed after the client conversation:**
  We agreed to move Case 3 from high-risk (Annex I) to limited risk / transparency. The reasoning: GSR-driven type-approval conformity assessment is a separate regulatory track from AI Act Annex I classification, and shouldn't be assumed to trigger Annex I on its own without confirming the product legislation actually designates this specific feature as a regulated "safety component" under the AI Act's cross-reference rules. Given the system processes data locally, shares nothing externally, and already discloses via the manual and dashboard icon, the transparency-only profile fits better absent that confirmation. My original call was the more conservative, defensible starting position, but overstated the risk once we talked through the Annex I trigger conditions specifically.

- **Borderline arguments or counter-arguments per case:**
  Case 3 remains the standout borderline case. Argument for high-risk: it's a legally-relevant vehicle safety feature in a type-approved product. Argument for limited risk / transparency (the one we landed on): local-only processing, no data leaves the vehicle, no evaluative decision made about the driver by a third party, and disclosure already meets Article 50-style expectations. The Annex I reading should still be flagged to a legal team rather than dismissed outright — it depends on how the vehicle's specific type-approval documentation characterizes this feature.
