# Phase 1 — Hidden Client Brief (Private Answer Key)

*Author: Akansha. This section stays private — not shared with partner.*

| Case | Client brief for partner | Intended category | Why you chose it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | An online higher-ed platform running remote-proctored exams wants to reduce exam misconduct. They're looking at a webcam-based add-on that scores each student's engagement and stress levels in real time during the exam and flags anything unusual to the invigilator team for follow-up. Most flags get a quick human look before anyone's contacted. | **Prohibited** | Real-time inference of emotional state (stress, engagement) from facial/behavioral signal, applied to students in an education setting — squarely Article 5(1)(f). The human review step happens *after* the inference and only on flagged cases, which is the trap: it looks like adequate oversight but doesn't cure a banned practice, since the prohibition attaches to the inference itself, not to what's done with its output. No medical or safety exception applies to exam-integrity monitoring. |
| **2** | A national recruitment company wants to help large employers screen candidates for engineering and technical vacancies. The AI system should analyse CVs, application responses, relevant qualifications, and work-history information to rank applicants against the requirements of a vacancy. Recruiters would receive a ranked shortlist and they would review the shortlist and could override the ranking before any candidate is rejected or invited to interview. | **High-risk** | Falls under Annex III(4)(a) — recruitment, candidate filtering, and ranking of applicants. Deliberately designed with a genuine oversight feature: recruiter review and override happen *before* any reject/invite decision, not after. This makes it a "well-designed high-risk system" case rather than a red flag — the point is that Annex III classification still applies even when the design is good; good design changes the decision (approve with controls) but not the category. |
| **3** | A skincare brand wants a friendlier shopping experience on their site. They're building a chat assistant, given a first name and a casual tone, to handle order and product questions so it feels less like "submitting a ticket" and more like chatting with someone on the team. It only hands off to a human if a customer specifically asks for one. | **Limited risk / transparency** | No Annex III trigger — pure customer service. The deliberate design choice to give the assistant a human name and casual tone, specifically so it doesn't feel automated, is the exact scenario Article 50 disclosure obligations are aimed at: a reasonable user isn't likely to realize it's AI absent a clear signal. Chosen so the transparency issue would only surface on close reading, not from an obvious "chatbot" framing. |
| **4** | A grocery e-commerce company wants to increase average order size. They're adding a "you might also like" module to the homepage that surfaces suggestions based on what a shopper has bought or browsed before. Nothing else about the account or checkout flow changes. | **Minimal risk** | Standard behavioral product recommender — no Annex III area, no biometric or emotion element, no individually consequential decision (it's a merchandising nudge, not an eligibility, pricing, or employment outcome). Chosen as the deliberately unremarkable control case, to test whether a reviewer can correctly identify "no AI Act obligations" rather than over-flagging every AI system as risky by default. |
