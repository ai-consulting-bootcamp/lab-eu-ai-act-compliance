# Phase 4/5 — Debrief

## Intended vs. inferred classification

| Case | Intended category (answer key) | Inferred category (blind review) | Match? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prohibited | Prohibited | Yes |
| 2 | High-risk | High-risk | Yes |
| 3 | Limited risk / transparency | Limited risk / transparency | Yes |
| 4 | Minimal risk | Minimal risk | Yes |

## Where the hidden scenario worked well

- Case 1's "safety monitoring" framing successfully disguised the real use
  (performance/promotion input) — the tell only surfaces once you read past
  the stated purpose to what the score actually feeds.
- Case 4's "AI makes decisions" framing tried to bait a high-risk read, but
  the presence of a full human override and the absence of any
  personal/Annex III data made the minimal-risk call clear on inspection.

## Where it was too vague / could be tightened

- Case 2 didn't specify whether recruiters could override a "not a fit"
  auto-rejection before it's sent — that ambiguity is realistic for a real
  discovery call, but a sharper brief would make the reviewer explicitly
  ask about it rather than assume no override exists.
- Case 3's disclosure gap (small "beta" label) was almost too subtle; a
  reviewer skimming quickly could miss that this is the actual compliance
  issue rather than the chatbot's existence itself.

## What would change after a real client conversation

- Case 1: client pushback would likely be "but it's for their wellbeing" —
  the closing note is that intent doesn't cure the Article 5 prohibition;
  the redesign (opt-in surveys + objective ops signals) is the only path
  that keeps the underlying business goal without the banned mechanism.
- Case 2: client would likely ask to keep the six-week timeline — the
  answer is that the mandatory human-review control is the one item that
  cannot be compressed, since it's the difference between "approve with
  controls" and a system that's non-compliant from day one.
