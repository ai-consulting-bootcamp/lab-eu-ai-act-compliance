# Consulting Response — Client Briefs (labels removed)

These are the four scenarios as a partner/reviewer would receive them: no
category hints, presented like real discovery-call notes.

---

## Case 1

A mid-sized logistics company with a mostly hourly warehouse workforce wants
to reduce turnover and "catch burnout before it becomes a resignation." They
plan to roll out desk and floor cameras with a vendor's software that reads
facial expressions and voice tone during shifts and daily stand-up meetings,
producing a per-employee "engagement score" each week. Shift supervisors
would see the scores on a dashboard and use them as one input when deciding
who gets extra coaching, who's flagged for a performance conversation, and
who's considered for the next round of promotions. Employees are told the
cameras are "for safety monitoring" in their onboarding packet but not that
emotional/tonal analysis feeds into performance decisions. There is no
opt-out currently planned, and supervisors are not required to disclose the
score when discussing a coaching plan with an employee.

---

## Case 2

A national retail chain gets roughly 400 CVs a week for store and warehouse
roles and its two-person HR team can't keep up. They want a tool that
ingests resumes and a short online assessment, scores each candidate against
the job description, and automatically ranks them into "strong fit,"
"maybe," and "not a fit" tiers. Recruiters mostly only open the "strong fit"
folder before making outreach calls; lower-tier candidates get an automated
rejection email. The scoring model was trained on the company's past five
years of hiring outcomes, including which hires were retained past one
year. HR wants to launch within six weeks and hasn't yet decided whether
recruiters must review every "not a fit" result before the rejection goes
out.

## Case 3

A consumer electronics retailer wants to add a chat widget to its website
and app that can answer order-status questions, walk shoppers through
warranty claims, and recommend accessories, available 24/7. It's built on a
general-purpose language model with a retrieval layer over the company's
product and policy documents, and it can escalate to a live agent if it
detects frustration or a request outside its scope. Customers interact with
it by typing in a normal chat window with a friendly agent name and avatar;
nothing on the screen currently states outright that it's an AI system
rather than a person, though a small "beta support tool" label appears in
the corner. It doesn't make binding decisions (refunds still require a human
click), but it does have real conversations that shoppers may not realize
are automated.

---

## Case 4

A regional grocery chain wants to improve shelf availability and cut food
waste by forecasting daily demand per store per SKU, using historical sales,
local weather forecasts, and calendar effects (holidays, local events). The
output feeds an internal ordering dashboard that suggests reorder quantities
to store managers, who can accept, adjust, or override any suggestion before
it's submitted to the warehouse. The system only processes aggregate sales
and product data — no customer-level or employee-level data is used or
produced, and nothing about the tool is customer-facing.
