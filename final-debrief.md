## What Changed After the Client Discussion?

After comparing my classifications with Luise's intended answer key, I confirmed that **all four cases matched my first-pass classifications**: Case 1 was minimal risk, Case 2 was high-risk, Case 3 was prohibited, and Case 4 was limited risk / transparency.

There were no major misunderstandings about the intended use of the systems, so my final recommendations did not change. However, the discussion clarified why the operating details matter.

For **Case 1**, confirming that ticket routing cannot affect merchant access to financial services strengthened the minimal-risk conclusion, while I retained logging and a strict purpose boundary as safeguards.

For **Case 2**, the discussion reinforced that a manager making the final employment decision does not automatically make the system low-risk. Because the AI produces individual employee evaluations used in performance conversations, I kept the high-risk classification and strengthened the controls around human oversight, employee information, access restrictions, bias and accuracy testing, and logging.

For **Case 3**, the discussion confirmed that the vulnerability-related signals are intentionally used to increase sales pressure. My decision therefore remained **deny and redesign**. The architecture must change by removing age, insufficient-funds signals and appearance-based vulnerability from the targeting logic. A redesigned version should rely on neutral user preferences, include notification limits and opt-outs, and receive a fresh AI Act classification before launch.

For **Case 4**, the risk call remained limited risk / transparency. The architecture did not require a fundamental redesign, but I made the disclosure and safety layers more explicit: users should know they are interacting with AI, generated theories should be distinguishable from factual episode information, and appropriate source, spoiler, moderation and logging controls should be maintained.

Overall, the client discussion **confirmed the classifications but refined the controls and deployment boundaries**. It showed that the final AI Act recommendation depends not only on what the model can do, but also on the data used, how the output affects people, where human review occurs, and how the system is operated in practice.

## Final Consulting Takeaway

The four cases demonstrate that similar AI technologies can lead to very different regulatory outcomes depending on their intended purpose and impact. Minimal-risk systems can generally proceed with proportionate operational safeguards; transparency systems need clear disclosure; high-risk employment uses require meaningful oversight and documented controls; and prohibited functionality must be removed rather than relying on downstream human review.
