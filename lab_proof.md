# Phase 3 | AI Act Approval Pack

## Executive summary — Can we launch?


**Conditional yes for SkyWays and Cedar & Finch, yes for Northline’s pilot, and no for BrightPath’s current monetization design.** The four cases span operational forecasting, customer-facing generative AI, employment decisions, and behavioral targeting in a wellbeing app. The key distinction is the system’s intended use and effect on people—not simply whether it uses AI or includes a human reviewer.

|---|---|---|
| SkyWays | Limited risk with transparency obligations | Approve with controls |

## 1. SkyWays — customer support and marketing video

**Client Brief:** A vendor-provided assistant answers booking questions in natural language, reads booking details and hands complicated refund requests to existing staff; it cannot approve or deny refunds. Marketing also plans to use the vendor’s video generator for social clips showing composite travellers at destinations, with a human editor checking claims. The airline asks what customers should be told and what labels belong on clips.

**Category:** Limited risk with transparency obligations. The support assistant answers customers but cannot decide refunds. The video generator creates synthetic marketing content.

**Architecture and roles:** A customer starts a support chat; the vendor assistant retrieves approved help content and booking details, answers routine questions, and hands complex refunds to a human agent. The agent decides the refund. The video generator creates clips for an editor to review. The vendor is likely provider of both tools; SkyWays is the deployer. Confirm the roles if SkyWays brands or substantially changes either system.

**Compliance and decision — Approve with controls:** Tell users they are interacting with AI at first contact unless that is obvious. The provider must machine-readably mark synthetic video. SkyWays must visibly disclose a clip if it constitutes a deepfake; assess whether composite people or scenes could falsely appear authentic. Keep the assistant out of refund decisions, provide escalation, protect booking data, and monitor vendor performance. Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026.

## 2. Cedar & Finch — recruitment and promotion workflow

**Client Brief:** A vendor platform reads job applications, CVs, availability and structured work-sample results, then ranks applicants; recruiters usually interview only the top fifth but can override with a reason. The same platform compares performance and training records to suggest employees for promotion; a store or regional manager makes the final decision. The retailer sets criteria and supplies data; the vendor hosts and updates the model. HR assumes the contract makes the vendor solely responsible.

**Category:** High-risk under Annex III employment use cases. The system filters and ranks job applicants and recommends employees for promotion. Interviewing only the top fifth makes the ranking consequential; a human override does not remove that influence.

**Architecture and roles:** On application or promotion cycle, the vendor system scores candidates or flags promotion matches using job-related evidence. A trained recruiter or manager reviews the underlying evidence, can override the recommendation, and records the reason. The system outputs a shortlist or recommendation, never an automatic rejection or promotion. The vendor is likely the provider; Cedar & Finch is the deployer and employer. A contract cannot transfer the retailer’s statutory duties.

**Compliance and decision — Approve with controls:** Before launch, require provider documentation, risk management, data governance, logging, quality controls, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity and conformity assessment as applicable. Cedar & Finch must follow the provider’s instructions, assign competent human oversight, monitor use, retain logs under its control for at least six months, and inform affected workers and their representatives before workplace use. Notify affected applicants/workers that the system is used. Validate job relevance, data representativeness, bias and accessibility; provide a route to challenge outcomes and complete GDPR and employment-law reviews, including a DPIA where required. Annex III obligations apply from 2 December 2027 under the current timeline; use the lead time to prepare, not to skip controls.

## 3. Northline Markets — store-level fresh-food ordering

**Client Brief:** After stores over-ordered fresh food, a service combines three years of sales, supplier lead times, weather, promotions and local-event calendars to suggest orders by store and product. It does not use customer profiles or employee records; buyers receive the suggestions. Store managers can edit before orders are placed, and overrides are recorded for future updates. The CFO asks whether AI alone means the pilot must pause.

**Category:** Minimal risk on the facts provided. It forecasts inventory needs and recommends quantities; it does not assess or make decisions about individuals or fall into a listed high-risk use.

**Architecture and roles:** At each planning cycle, a forecast model combines sales, lead times, weather, promotions and local events to suggest store/product quantities. Buyers or store managers review and edit before ordering. Keep forecast, input freshness, model version, overrides and error records. Northline is the deployer if it uses a supplier’s product; the supplier is likely provider. Northline may be provider if it develops or has the system developed and puts it into service under its own name.

**Compliance and decision — Approve:** No specific high-risk or Article 50 obligation is apparent. Pilot with proportionate data-quality, access, security and forecast-monitoring controls. Reassess if the purpose expands to worker monitoring, personal profiling, or safety-critical automated actions. Check supplier-data rights and any separate food-safety or consumer claims.

## 4. BrightPath — wellbeing prompts linked to subscription conversion

**Client Brief:** For adult EU users, after several mood entries, voice notes or connected sleep data, the app estimates when a guided-programme prompt may feel timely. The product team found sign-ups rise after poor-sleep streaks or entries saying the user is struggling, so the system chooses prompt timing and wording. A one-tap trial converts into an annual subscription unless canceled, at the same price for everyone. 

**Category:** **Potentially prohibited, but not conclusively established from the available brief.** The system uses poor-sleep or distress signals to time and tailor sales prompts. Article 5 requires specific elements, including material distortion of behavior and significant harm; the vulnerability route is limited to vulnerability linked to age, disability, or a specific social or economic situation. The brief is truncated after “The client says the prompt is labelled…”, so that missing detail and the actual user journey could change the assessment.

**Architecture and roles:** The current flow triggers on mood, voice or sleep signals and selects persuasive sales timing/copy for an auto-renewing annual subscription. BrightPath is likely provider of the in-app feature and deployer; any model or analytics supplier may be provider of its component. Confirm who determines the feature’s purpose and controls the optimization.

**Compliance and decision — Deny and redesign:** Do not launch this distress-targeted sales design while the Article 5 threshold and user impact remain unresolved. A lawful alternative is to let users choose neutral wellbeing reminders, separate support prompts from sales, and present any paid plan in a distinct flow with clear price, renewal and cancellation terms and an explicit purchase choice. Do not use mood, voice or sleep signals to target conversion. Assess GDPR requirements because these signals may reveal health data, and review consumer-protection rules for the trial and auto-renewal. This is a precautionary launch decision, not a finding that the incomplete facts conclusively prove an Article 5 prohibition.
