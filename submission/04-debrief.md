## Where the Hidden Scenarios Worked Well

All eight briefs successfully disguised their category well enough to require genuine reasoning rather than obvious pattern-matching, while still being fair — every case was classified correctly once properly reasoned through, meaning the scenarios were realistic without being impossible. The Case 1/Case 3 pairing (same underlying emotion/state-detection technology, disguised QA purpose vs. disclosed safety purpose) was particularly effective: it forced a real distinction between purpose and disclosure rather than pattern-matching "cameras watching people = bad."

## Where a Scenario Was Too Vague or Too Obvious

The driver-monitoring brief (Case 3) was the one genuine miss: Anand's first-pass read was high-risk, not limited risk/transparency. This wasn't a flaw in the brief being "unfair" — the scenario legitimately involves safety-critical monitoring for an automotive product, which makes a high-risk first instinct reasonable. It surfaced the same subtlety flagged during my own reasoning process on this case: good intent and a genuine safety purpose don't automatically mean *no* obligations apply, but they also don't automatically escalate a case to high-risk either — the correct test is whether the specific Annex III list is actually touched, not whether the use case *feels* serious or safety-related. Anand aligned with the limited risk/transparency classification once we walked through why this falls outside Annex III (no employment, credit, law enforcement, etc. decision being made about a person) while still requiring Article 50 disclosure because it involves monitoring a person's state.

## Self-Correction Notes (Own Reasoning Process)

Classifications on Anand's four cases were directionally correct on first pass in all four instances. Two corrections were needed to the *reasoning*, not the label:

- **Case 1:** initially cited "Annex III" when the correct basis was Article 5 (prohibited practices are governed by Article 5, not Annex III, which is the high-risk list). The mechanism was also refined from "state inference" to the more accurate "social scoring."
- **Case 2:** initially framed the high-risk trigger as depending on whether human oversight would be overridden. Corrected to recognize that Annex III recruitment/screening is the trigger on its own, independent of oversight quality — oversight quality is a separate question about whether a required control is genuinely functioning.

This reinforced a general lesson applied consistently across the portfolio: **risk classification and human-oversight quality are two separate judgments.** Weak oversight doesn't change a case's classification (Case 2 in my own set); strong oversight doesn't create a low-risk classification on its own either (Case 4 in my own set). Both need to be assessed independently.

## How the Recommendation Would Change After the Client Conversation

As the "client," Anand accepted the approval pack as presented — no requested redesigns or challenges to the decisions (deny/redesign on Case 1, approve with controls on Cases 2 and 3, approve on Case 4). The one live adjustment was on the reasoning behind Case 3's classification, not the decision itself: Anand initially treated the driver-monitoring case as high-risk given the safety-critical framing, and aligned to limited risk/transparency once we walked through why safety-motivated intent doesn't itself trigger Annex III, and why Article 50 disclosure is the correct — and still real — obligation here rather than no obligation at all or a high-risk designation.

## Consultant's Closing Note

The main value of this exercise wasn't discovering a wrong classification — every case ultimately landed correctly — but confirming *why* each one was correct well enough to defend it under live questioning. The one case that needed real-time discussion (driver monitoring, Case 3) is exactly the kind of case a real client would push back on, since "this is a safety feature" is a completely reasonable instinct that happens to point toward the wrong tier. Being able to explain, in the moment, why safety purpose changes the *outcome* (not prohibited) but doesn't erase the *obligation* (still requires disclosure) is the actual consulting skill this lab was testing — not just producing four correct labels.
